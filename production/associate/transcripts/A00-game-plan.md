## In this episode

SAM: Welcome to Pass the Architect, Associate Foundations. This is episode zero, the game plan.

ALEX: No Claude features today?

SAM: Not one. By the end you'll be able to describe the paper and where its marks sit, pace it against a clock that counts down, and handle items that list statements with a dropdown each. You'll have a four-step method for reading any item in plain business language, tested on the three official sample questions.

ALEX: Because marks go for reasons that have nothing to do with Claude. Picking one answer when the item wanted two, or trusting an answer because it sounded sure.

SAM: That second one is where we start. [sting]

## Cold open: the confident citation

SAM: An item before anything else, and it is the first official sample from the exam guide. An associate asks Claude to summarise a new regulation, and Claude produces a confident summary citing a specific subsection number. Before sending the summary to the compliance team, what is the most appropriate action?

SAM: Option A, send it as is, since Claude expressed high confidence. Option B, verify the cited subsection against the official regulation text before sharing. Option C, ask Claude to rate its own confidence, and send it if the rating is high. Option D, reword the summary to sound more formal, then send it.

ALEX: C. It's quick, it's a real check, and if Claude says it's unsure I know to dig in. That's what I'd do on a busy afternoon.

SAM: Hold that. By the end of the method you should be able to say exactly why it loses.

ALEX: Then the shape of the thing. What am I walking into?

SAM: Sixty items in a hundred and twenty minutes. Multiple choice and multiple response, and every item says how many to select. It is proctored by Pearson VUE, online or at a test centre.

ALEX: And the pass mark?

SAM: A scaled seven hundred and twenty, on a range of one hundred to one thousand. It is criterion-referenced: you are measured against a fixed standard, not against other candidates. The score report shows percent correct by domain, but only the total decides pass or fail, so a strong domain can carry a weak one.

ALEX: Who is it written for?

SAM: Someone like you. A professional who uses Claude as a working tool, builds Projects, and finishes tasks with little guidance. Someone who balances quality, efficiency and cost, knows the limits, and hands technical work to Architects and Developers. No code, and no A P I.

ALEX: So "escalate" can be a right answer.

SAM: When the work really is beyond your role, yes. Escalating is not the same as giving up, and sample three shows the difference.

SAM: Now the weights. Output Evaluation and Validation, twenty-one percent, about thirteen items. Workflow Integration and Solution Design, sixteen, about ten. Governance, Risk and Responsible Use, fifteen, about nine. Prompting and Task Execution, fourteen, about eight. Product and Model Selection, and Configuration and Knowledge Management, twelve each, about seven apiece. Troubleshooting and Optimization, ten, about six. The guide calls the weights approximate, so treat the counts as a guide.

ALEX: Checking Claude's work outweighs prompting by half again.

SAM: And with governance it is over a third of the paper. The exam cares less about coaxing a good answer out of Claude than about knowing whether to trust it, and what you may put in. [sting]

## The clock and the dropdowns

ALEX: Pacing.

SAM: About one minute fifty an item, which leaves ten minutes at the end for flagged items. The clock counts down in hours and minutes, so the checkpoints are time left. Item twenty at one hour twenty-three left. Item forty at forty-seven minutes left. Item sixty at ten minutes left.

ALEX: One minute fifty feels generous. Most of these are a short paragraph. I'd rather spend time where I need it and ignore the clock.

SAM: Plenty of items will take under a minute; bank that. The checkpoints aren't a speed limit. They are three glances that tell you whether a slow item early on is eating time a later item needs.

SAM: And the tell is your own behaviour: if you've reread a stem three times, pick your best answer, flag it, and move on. A blank never scores, and the guide describes no penalty for a wrong answer.

ALEX: And the item formats?

SAM: The guide names two. Multiple choice, one answer. Multiple response, where the item tells you how many. Read that number first, then judge each option on its own: true here, or not. The guide doesn't mention partial credit, so treat one wrong pick as losing the item.

SAM: A third shape comes from the Pearson VUE screen at the Architect exams: four or five statements, each with its own dropdown of two or three choices. Every statement is its own small judgement, and none is a throwaway.

ALEX: And nothing says which domain I'm in?

SAM: Nothing. Every item is a workplace story.

SAM: Pause here and answer before we go on. A dropdown item. A marketing lead has a customer newsletter drafted by Claude. For each statement, choose "check against a source" or "a normal read-through is enough". One, a market-share percentage Claude gave for a competitor. Two, the tone and length of the opening paragraph. Three, a quote attributed to a named industry analyst. Four, three subject lines to choose from. [pause]

ALEX: Check, read-through, check, read-through.

SAM: All four right. The percentage and the quote are specific facts a reader will rely on, and specific-looking detail is what a model can produce fluently and wrongly. Tone and subject lines are judgement calls; there is no source to check them against.

ALEX: Then why not check everything? Safer.

SAM: Safer, but slower, and not what the exam rewards. The guide's word is "when": determine when human review or extra verification is required.

SAM: The tell is a specific, checkable claim: a number, a name, a quote, a citation. Those get checked. Style gets read. [sting]

## The four-step method

ALEX: Now the method.

SAM: Four steps, in plain words. One, the ask. Read the last line first: most appropriate, best fits, first step, NOT. And how many to pick.

ALEX: Two?

SAM: The stakes. Who receives this, and what rule is in play? A compliance team, a customer, a data policy, a budget, a volume. Those words decide the answer, and they sit mid-stem, where the eye skips.

ALEX: Three?

SAM: The proportionate step. What would a careful colleague do that lets the work go ahead safely? Not the maximum, and not nothing. The step sized to the stakes.

ALEX: And four?

SAM: Strike the families. Five kinds of wrong answer come back again and again. Trusting the model's confidence. Overspending: the biggest model, or a full review for a low-stakes draft. Asking the model to promise what a control should do. Abandoning a task that has a safe route. And polish: rewording or reformatting, which changes nothing about whether it's right.

ALEX: Ask, stakes, step, strike.

SAM: That's the method. Now the other two samples. [sting]

## Samples two and three

SAM: Pause here and answer before we go on. The second official sample. An associate needs to generate a high volume of short customer-reply drafts where speed and cost matter more than deep reasoning. Which choice best fits the task? A, use the most capable, highest-cost model for every reply to maximise quality. B, use a faster, lower-cost model suited to straightforward, high-volume tasks. C, disable all product features to reduce cost. D, switch to a different A I platform. [pause]

ALEX: My gut says A. These go to customers, and a bad reply costs more than a few pence of model time.

SAM: A real concern, and on a complaint from your biggest client you'd want the strongest model and a careful read. But walk the steps. The ask: best fits the task. The stakes: high volume, short replies, and the stem itself says speed and cost matter more than deep reasoning.

ALEX: So the stem has already made the trade-off for me.

SAM: It has. B: a faster, lower-cost model for straightforward, high-volume work, keeping the most capable one for complex reasoning. A overspends, in time as well as money. C and D never touch the trade-off.

SAM: The tell: when the stem ranks the priorities, "matter more than", believe it. Don't import a quality requirement it never stated.

SAM: Pause here and answer before we go on. The third official sample. A project manager wants to upload a spreadsheet containing customer names and account numbers so Claude can analyse trends. Organisational policy restricts sharing regulated personal data. What is the most appropriate action? A, upload the file as is, since the analysis is internal. B, remove or anonymise the personal identifiers before uploading, consistent with policy. C, upload the file but instruct Claude not to retain it. D, skip the analysis entirely. [pause]

ALEX: C. The trends are the point. Tell it not to keep the file and get on with it.

SAM: Which family is that?

ALEX: Asking the model to promise. But my company's account has its own data settings. It's not as if I'm pasting it into a stranger's website.

SAM: Your organisation's settings matter. But the stem names the control: the policy restricts sharing the data. An instruction typed into the chat arrives after the data has been shared. The guide's rationale says it outright: an instruction not to retain does not satisfy the policy control.

SAM: So B. The trends survive without names and account numbers, so anonymising lets the analysis go ahead without exposing protected data. A breaks the policy. D abandons a task that has a safe route, and that is the difference from escalating: you escalate work beyond your role, not work with a safe route.

ALEX: And the tell?

SAM: "Policy restricts" plus identifiers in a file. The fix happens before the upload, in the data, not afterwards, in a request. [sting]

## Cold open: the verdict

SAM: Back to the confident citation. Alex, you said C, ask Claude to rate its own confidence.

ALEX: Let me walk it. The ask: most appropriate action before sending. The stakes: a compliance team, and a specific subsection number.

SAM: And the step?

ALEX: Open the regulation and read the subsection. Which is B.

SAM: B. A specific-looking citation is exactly what a model can invent, and a compliance team will act on it. Checking the official text is the diligence step. A trusts the confidence. C trusts it twice: a self-rating is still the model's own view, not evidence. D is polish.

ALEX: One more go for C. If Claude says it's only sixty percent sure, I've learned something.

SAM: A low rating is a fair cue to dig in. But a high one tells you nothing, and the answer here was already confident. The guide says self-reported confidence is not a reliable accuracy signal.

SAM: The tell: "confident" next to a citation, a figure or a name, heading to someone who will rely on it. Check it at the source. [sting]

## Using the series

ALEX: How do I use the rest of the series?

SAM: In order, one domain an episode from one to seven. Two, checking what Claude gives you, is the biggest domain and the longest episode. Eight, the final drill, is for the night before, and nine, exam day, for the morning.

ALEX: And when you say pause?

SAM: Pause, and say your answer out loud before you hear ours. Call every option in a choose-two, and every statement in a dropdown. Recalling an answer does more than hearing it twice. [sting]

## Recap: the exam tells

SAM: One more pass. Pause and name the four parts of the game plan: the paper, the clock, the formats and the method. [pause]

ALEX: Sixty items in a hundred and twenty minutes, pass at seven hundred and twenty, checking outputs biggest at twenty-one percent. One twenty-three, forty-seven and ten left at items twenty, forty and sixty. Multiple choice, multiple response, dropdowns. Ask, stakes, step, strike.

SAM: Now as exam tells. If you've reread a stem three times, pick your best answer, flag it and move on. Never leave a blank.

SAM: If an answer is confident and carries a specific citation, figure or name, check it at the source. A self-rating is not a check.

ALEX: If only tone or style is in question, a read-through is enough. Sized to the stakes, not the maximum.

SAM: If the stem ranks speed and cost above deep reasoning, take the faster, lower-cost model.

ALEX: If a policy restricts the data, fix the data before it goes in. A request not to retain it is not a control.

SAM: And if an option abandons the task, look for the one that makes it safe instead.
